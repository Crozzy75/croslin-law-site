Probate

It’s not easy losing a loved one. We help take the pressure off by walking you through probate, handling the paperwork, and helping make sure your loved one’s estate is administered properly.

Named executor? Deadlines start soon after you’re appointed.

How we can help

  • Opening an estate and petitioning for probate
  • Guiding executors through their duties
  • Valuing assets, notifying creditors, and paying valid debts
  • Handling small estate affidavits and summary administration
  • Distributing assets and closing estates
  • Disputing a will, or defending against a contested will

What to expect

Probate starts with the basics: where your loved one lived, what assets and debts they had, whether they had a will, and who will serve as executor. Once we have those answers, we file a petition to open the probate case and begin administering the estate. The court then sets a hearing, and we guide you through each step from there.

Common questions

Does everything have to go through probate?

Often, yes. Whether or not there is a will, property owned in the deceased person’s name alone generally has to go through probate. Some property doesn’t, such as accounts with a named beneficiary, like life insurance and retirement accounts, jointly owned property that passes to the survivor, and property held in a trust. Smaller estates may also qualify for a simpler process. Which applies depends on what the person owned and how it was titled.

I’ve been named executor. What do I have to do, and am I personally responsible?

If you are named executor, which Oklahoma law calls a personal representative, you will need to act promptly, because deadlines start soon after you are appointed. Executors are required to locate and value the deceased person’s assets, notify creditors of the death, and pay valid debts and expenses from the estate. Executors are generally not personally responsible for the estate’s debts. However, an executor who breaches a duty, such as the duty to act honestly and in the estate’s best interest, can be held personally liable. Getting guidance early helps you avoid mistakes.

A family member just died. What do I do first?

If a loved one has just died, one of the first things to do is find the will, if there is one. Whoever has it must deliver it to the court, or to the executor named in it, within thirty days of learning of the death. Next comes a petition to start the probate case, which includes details such as where and when the person died, whether there was a will, the probable value of the estate, and who the heirs may be. The court then sets a hearing to decide what happens next. Smaller estates may qualify for a simpler process. Probate has many steps, so it helps to talk to an attorney early.

What happens to the debts of someone who died?

When a person dies, their debts are generally paid from their estate, not by their family. In probate, the executor must search for creditors and give them notice. Creditors then have a chance to show they have a valid claim, and the executor and the judge decide which debts are valid and pay them. If the estate has more debt than assets, there may not be enough to pay everyone. In that case, the money is divided under set rules, and some creditors may not be paid in full, depending on their priority. Family members usually aren’t personally responsible for these debts, though there are exceptions, such as someone who cosigned a loan.

I am involved in a will dispute. What can I expect?

Disputes over the validity of a will are generally difficult to prove. A person may dispute a will only on specific grounds identified by law. Courts are generally unlikely to revoke a will for fraud, menace, duress, or undue influence. There may be other grounds, such as the will not being properly signed and witnessed. A challenge after a will has been admitted to probate generally must be filed within three months, so it’s important to talk to an attorney early.
