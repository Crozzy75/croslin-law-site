Adoption

Adoptions create a permanent legal family relationship. We can help families complete the process from start to finish, or help parents fight adoptions without consent.

Filing or contesting an adoption? The sooner you have a plan, the stronger your position.

How we can help

  • Stepparent adoption
  • Private adoption
  • Gestational surrogacy agreements
  • Defending against adoptions without consent
  • Adoptions involving tribal membership (ICWA)

What to expect

Adoption starts with understanding the facts of your case. Once we know what the child is going through, who wants to adopt, and where the birth parents stand, we develop a plan that works for you. From there, we prepare the paperwork, handle notice and consents, and guide you to the final hearing. If you are a parent facing an adoption you did not agree to, we prepare to contest it.

Common questions

Do I have to have the consent of both parents?

Generally, yes. Oklahoma law requires the consent of both parents, with specific exceptions. A court can find that a parent’s consent isn’t required in certain situations. For example, a parent’s consent may not be required if, for 12 consecutive months out of the 14 months before the adoption petition was filed, the parent willfully failed to support the child, according to their financial ability, or failed to maintain a substantial and positive relationship with the child. The person asking to adopt must prove the exception, and even then the court must separately find that the adoption is in the child’s best interests.

Who can adopt?

To adopt in Oklahoma, a person generally must be at least 21 years old. Single people and married couples can both adopt. The person seeking to adopt must also show the court that they are fit to adopt, typically by showing adequate health, finances, and living arrangements for the child. You don’t need to be related to the child to adopt, although adoptions by non-relatives often take longer.

What is a stepparent adoption?

A stepparent adoption is when the spouse of a child’s parent adopts that child. The parent keeps their rights, and the child gains a second legal parent. Because the child is already living in the family, some requirements are relaxed, and a court may waive the home study in some cases. The child’s other birth parent still has to be addressed: either they consent, or the court finds their consent isn’t required. That is often the most important part of the case.

Do I have to have a home study?

Usually, yes, but not always. A home study reviews the home and background of the person adopting, and Oklahoma law generally requires one. A court may waive it in some cases, such as when the child has lived with the person adopting for at least a year and no one in the home has a record of certain offenses, such as child abuse, neglect, or domestic violence. Waiver is generally limited to stepparents and immediate relatives.

My child could be a member of a tribe. How does that affect an adoption?

A tribe’s involvement can make an adoption more complicated. If a child is a member of a tribe, or may be eligible for membership, that must be disclosed to the court. The tribe is then entitled to notice, as are certain other parties, such as the Bureau of Indian Affairs. Federal and Oklahoma law also set placement preferences, which can favor extended family or other tribal members unless the court finds good cause. A tribe may also intervene to dispute a proposed placement, which can lengthen the case and increase its cost.

I received a notice that someone wants to adopt a child without my consent. What can I do?

If you have received notice that someone wants to adopt a child without your consent, don’t ignore it. You have the right to respond and to contest the request, and deadlines apply, so it’s important to talk with an attorney right away. A court can find that a parent’s consent isn’t required only in narrow circumstances, and the person asking to adopt has the burden of proving that those circumstances exist. Even then, the court must separately find that the adoption is in the child’s best interests.
